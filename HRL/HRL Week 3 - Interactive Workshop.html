Experimental designs: establishing cause and effect

Why would a scary place make a good date?

▶ Watch: Misattribution of Arousal

Predict first (1974 bridge study). A researcher approached men on a high, swaying bridge or on a low, solid bridge and offered a phone number. Which men called more often?

About half of the men on the high bridge called, against roughly one in eight on the low bridge (Dutton & Aron, 1974).
What is the role of theory?Theory (misattribution of arousal) predicts the result in advance: people may read the racing heart caused by fear as attraction. Without theory you only have a curious difference.
How is the experiment designed?The cause being studied is arousal (high vs low bridge). The effect is interest in the researcher (for example, calling back). Everything else, such as the approach, is kept the same.
How solid are the conclusions?Men were not randomly assigned to a bridge, so the two groups may differ (for example in daring). That is self-selection. A true experiment would randomly assign people to, say, a horror or a non-horror film and then measure attraction.

Describing the limitations

Is self check-in faster than reception check-in?

1 · What?The sample is not randomised: guests who choose self check-in may differ from guests who choose reception.
2 · So what?Self check-in guests may be quicker with technology, so the effect is inflated. If all guests used self check-in, its average time would be higher.
3 · Now what?Test the assumption (measure self check-in with the other type of guest too) and/or adjust for the effect.

Version B (invented example)

We used observation and review analysis because we needed to see waiting times as they happen and to hear how guests experience them. We observed 12 hours spread over weekday mornings and Friday evenings and timed every 3rd check-in (n = 28). We analysed the 100 most recent online reviews. Waiting time was measured in minutes from joining the queue to receiving the key; reviews were scored on the SERVQUAL dimensions. Two team members used a standard observation sheet and a coding scheme (see appendices). Limitations: the observation covered only two weeks, staff knew they were observed, and review writers are self-selected.

Quantitative

  • Numbers and graphs; statistical analysis
  • When: hypothesis testing or precise measurement
  • Designs: experimental, descriptive (large scale)
  • Methods: observations, surveys, measurement equipment, content analysis (e.g. reviews)

Qualitative

  • Words; categorisation and interpretation
  • When: explore ideas, understand experiences, insight in a specific context
  • Designs: exploratory, descriptive (small scale), case studies
  • Methods: observations, netnography, interviews, focus groups

Deciding on the sampling

How many do you need to measure?

1 · Population

Everyone or everything you want to say something about. For example: customers, employees and managers, reviews (already a sample), opening hours in a week, or the times an activity happens in a week.

2 · Sample

The part of the population you actually measure. You use it to say something about the whole population.

3 · How large should the sample be? The course rule of thumb

Population under 100

at least 50%

40 employees: at least 20. In a small group, each person you miss weighs heavily.

Population 100 to 1000

no fixed rule

400 guests a week: measure as many as you can.

Population over 1000

around 300

5,000 guests a year: around 300. In a large group, the number matters more than the percentage.

These numbers are often not realistic, so aim for as many as possible. In your report, state how many you measured, out of how many, and how you selected them.

Reporting outcomes in the organisation analysis

Where does it go?

The basic rules
  • Appendices: full protocols, coding schemes, questions, survey, and the complete data (transcripts, frequencies, observation results).
  • Main report: concise yet complete. Mention the numbers, make full comparisons, include all data that lead to your conclusions.
  • No double information.
  • Three numbers or fewer: use text. Tables and charts when they are more insightful and faster to understand.
  • Introduce numbers with a sentence and conclude with a sentence.
